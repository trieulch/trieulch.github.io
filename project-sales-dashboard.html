Sales & Products Dashboard

Power BI, DAX · data modelling · ~56K records

How is a bike retailer really doing, where do its sales come from, and which products keep customers coming back? A three-page Power BI report built for managers who want answers in a few clicks.

The business problem

The company sells bikes, accessories and clothing in four countries. Managers had the raw sales tables but no single place to see how the business was doing. Every question, such as "how did Australia do last quarter?" or "which products sell best?", meant another spreadsheet.

So the goal was a report that answers the everyday questions on its own: how big is the business, where is it growing, and which products drive sales and repeat purchases?

Try the report

The report is live below. Use the year buttons to filter, switch between Sales, Revenue, Orders and Customers at the top, and move between the three pages with the arrows at the bottom.

The data and the model

Heads up: this is a public sample dataset of a bike retailer, covering January 2015 to June 2017. The numbers are realistic but not from a real company.

The sales records sit in a fact table linked to lookup tables for products, customers, countries and dates, so every chart can be filtered by any of them. Two measures carry most of the report:

Sales   = price × quantity
Revenue = Sales − cost × quantity

"Revenue" in the report is what is left after product cost, so it works as gross profit.

What the report does

  • One switch for four measures. A field parameter lets the reader swap every chart between Sales, Revenue, Orders and Customers, instead of building four versions of each page.
  • Year filter that applies to every visual on the page.
  • Forecast on the monthly trend lines, so managers see where the next months are heading.
  • Smart narrative that writes the key change in plain words, such as the longest growth streak or the biggest monthly jump.
  • Month-over-month KPI card with an up or down arrow for the latest month.
  • Maps by continent and country, and a product table with star ratings and small trend lines for each subcategory.
  • Navigation buttons between the three pages, so it reads like a short story: overview, then customers, then products.

A few ratios come straight out of those cards. The business keeps about 42 cents of every dollar after product cost. An average order is worth about $990, which makes sense when most orders include a bike. And each customer placed 1.44 orders on average, so most people buy once.

The monthly trend

Sales were steady at roughly $0.5M to $0.7M a month through most of 2015, dipped in autumn, and jumped 73% in December 2015. The first half of 2016 was weak. From July 2016 sales climbed almost every month, reaching about $1.7M in June 2017, nearly three times January 2015.

Bike sales in the second half of 2016 were almost double the first half. July 2016 is also when accessories and clothing first went on sale, and when touring bikes were added.

2. Customer geography: where are the sales?

Three markets are close to each other, between $6.7M and $7.7M each. The United Kingdom sells less than half as much as any of them. That gap is the main question this page raises: is the UK a smaller market, or one the company has not pushed yet?

The page also shows each country's monthly trend with a forecast. The smart narrative points out that the United States had the longest growth streak, six months in a row from July 2016.

3. Products: what sells, and what brings people back?

Sales by category

Bikes bring in $23.6M, almost 95% of sales. Road bikes alone are $11.3M (45% of all sales) and mountain bikes $8.6M. The business is, in practice, a bike shop with a small add-on range.

Sales versus repeat purchases

The product table puts sales next to how often customers come back for the same subcategory. The two rankings are almost opposite:

People buy a bike once, but they come back for tires, tubes and bottles. Tires and Tubes sell more units than any other subcategory and have the highest retention at 30.5%, against 7.9% for road bikes.

So accessories are worth more than their 3.6% of sales suggests: they are the reason customers return. The recommendation was to bundle them with bikes, for example a road bike with a spare tube kit and a bottle cage at a small discount, and to remind buyers when it is time to replace their tires.

How sales and units move together

A scatter plot of every product shows two clear groups. Bikes sell in the hundreds of units but bring in $0.3M to $1.2M each. Accessories sell in the thousands of units but bring in under $0.1M each. Growing sales means selling more bikes; growing loyalty means selling more accessories.

Takeaways and next steps

  • A field parameter and a few well-chosen measures let one set of pages answer four different questions.
  • Sales nearly tripled between early 2015 and mid-2017, with the clearest step up from July 2016.
  • The United Kingdom is the obvious market to look into next.
  • Bikes make the money, but accessories bring customers back. The two should be sold together.
  • Next, I would add a returns page and a customer cohort view to measure retention over time rather than as a single rate.
