E-commerce Performance Analysis

MySQL, Python · A/B testing · 500K+ sessions, 1.3M+ pageviews

Where does an online store's traffic come from, where do visitors drop off, and which changes actually make money? An end-to-end SQL analysis of three years of website, order and refund data.

The business problem

The company is a young online toy store. It launched with one product, Mr. Fuzzy, and added three more over the next two years. Almost all of its visitors arrive through paid search ads, so every session costs money.

Marketing, the website team and management each had their own questions. Which ads are worth paying for? Why do so many visitors leave right away? Did the new pages and products help? Are customers coming back? So the question I wanted to answer is: where is the store losing visitors and money, and which changes win them back?

Each section below starts from one of those questions, answers it with SQL, and ends with what I would recommend.

The data

Heads up: this is a public training dataset for an online store. The numbers are realistic but not from a real company, so treat the recommendations as practice.

The data sits in a MySQL database with five tables:

TableWhat one row isSize
website_sessionsOne visit, with its traffic source, campaign and device500K+ rows
website_pageviewsOne page seen during a visit1.3M+ rows
ordersOne order, with its price and cost~32K rows
order_itemsOne product inside an order~40K rows
order_item_refundsOne refunded product~1.7K rows

A visitor who buys goes through the same pages every time:

/home (or a test landing page) → /products → /the-original-mr-fuzzy → /cart → /shipping → /billing → /thank-you

Two business rules shaped the analysis. Paid search costs about $2 per click, and marketing wants at least a 4% conversion rate from session to order before it spends more on a campaign.

1. Traffic: which ads are worth paying for?

In the first months, the gsearch nonbrand campaign brought in 97.4% of all traffic. The whole business depended on one campaign, so I checked whether it paid for itself. It converted at 2.96%, below the 4% target, so marketing lowered its bids. Weekly sessions then fell by about 42.5%, which showed how sensitive that traffic is to price.

Splitting the same campaign by device told a sharper story:

DeviceSessionsOrdersConversion rateCost per order (CPA)
Desktop3,9111463.73%$53.62
Mobile2,492240.96%$208.33

CPA is simply the click price divided by the conversion rate: $2 ÷ 3.73% ≈ $54. A desktop order costs about a quarter of a mobile one, so the recommendation was to move bid budget towards desktop rather than cut the campaign as a whole.

SELECT w_s.device_type,
       COUNT(DISTINCT w_s.website_session_id) AS sessions,
       COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id) AS orders,
       COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id) / COUNT(DISTINCT w_s.website_session_id) AS conversion_rate
FROM website_sessions w_s
LEFT JOIN orders o ON w_s.website_session_id = o.website_session_id
WHERE w_s.created_at < '2012-05-11'
  AND w_s.utm_source = 'gsearch' AND w_s.utm_campaign = 'nonbrand'
GROUP BY 1;

2. Website: where do visitors drop off?

The landing page test

Most visitors entered on the homepage, and 59.2% of them left without clicking anything (they "bounced"). The website team built a new landing page, /lander-1, and split traffic between the two pages. Over the same period:

Landing pageSessionsBouncedBounce rate
/home2,2611,31958.3%
/lander-12,3151,23253.2%

The new page kept about 5 more visitors in every 100. After all paid traffic was moved to it, I estimated roughly 200 extra orders over the following months, about 50 a month.

The conversion funnel

Next I followed every session through the checkout pages and measured how many people moved on at each step:

The billing page stood out. Visitors who reach it have already chosen a product and entered their address, yet 57% of them gave up there. That made it the best place to test a change.

The billing page test

The team built /billing-2 and showed it to half the visitors for about two months. To keep the test fair, I only counted sessions from the moment the new page went live:

Billing pageSessionsOrdersBilling → orderRevenue per billing session
/billing (old)65730045.7%$22.83
/billing-2 (new)65441062.7%$31.34

The new page converted 17 percentage points better and earned 37% more per billing session. That result supported rolling it out to every visitor.

SELECT pageview_url,
       COUNT(website_session_id) AS sessions,
       COUNT(order_id) AS orders,
       COUNT(order_id) / COUNT(website_session_id) AS conversion_rate
FROM (SELECT w_p.website_session_id, w_p.pageview_url, o.order_id
      FROM website_pageviews w_p
      LEFT JOIN orders o ON w_p.website_session_id = o.website_session_id
      WHERE w_p.website_pageview_id > 53550          -- first /billing-2 pageview
        AND w_p.pageview_url IN ('/billing', '/billing-2')
        AND w_p.created_at BETWEEN '2012-09-05' AND '2012-11-10') AS flagged
GROUP BY pageview_url;

3. Channels: is the store less dependent on one ad?

A second search engine, bsearch, was added later. On every device, gsearch converted better:

SourceDesktopMobile
gsearch4.52%1.28%
bsearch3.79%0.77%

So bsearch bids were lowered slightly, and its sessions dipped a little. More importantly, over time the share of traffic from brand searches, direct visits and organic search kept growing. Those visitors are free or cheap to bring in, which means customers were starting to look for the store by name.

4. Seasonality: when do people buy?

From one year to the next, sessions grew by 94% and orders by 157%, so the store was not just getting more visitors but turning more of them into buyers. Every year, traffic spiked in the weeks of Black Friday and Cyber Monday.

Two practical points came out of this. Customer support should be fully staffed on weekday office hours and in late November. And because conversion only partly moves with traffic (R² = 0.44), more sessions alone do not guarantee more orders.

5. Products: did new launches help?

This was the biggest part of the project. The store launched one product at a time, so each launch worked almost like a natural experiment: I could compare the months before and after it.

#ProductLaunched
1The Original Mr. FuzzyMarch 2012
2The Forever Love BearJanuary 2013
3The Birthday Sugar PandaDecember 2013
4The Hudson River Mini BearFebruary 2014

Sales and margin over time

Monthly revenue grew from about $3,000 in the first month to $144,800 in December 2014, nearly 50 times as much, an average of 29% a month. The gross margin stayed close to 61% on Mr. Fuzzy, so growth did not come from cutting prices.

By 2014, the newer products made up almost half of revenue:

That matters for risk. In 2012 the store depended on one product. Two years later, if one product had a problem, more than 40% of revenue would still be safe.

Launch 1: the Love Bear

The first question was whether a second product would simply take sales away from Mr. Fuzzy. Comparing the three months before and after the launch:

PeriodSessionsWent on from /productsChose Mr. FuzzyChose Love Bear
Before launch15,69572.3%72.3%-
After launch10,70976.6%62.1%14.4%

A few visitors who would have picked Mr. Fuzzy picked the Love Bear instead. But more visitors clicked through from the product page overall (+4.3 points), so the store gained buyers rather than just shuffling them. Conversion rose from 5.0% to 6.1% and revenue per session from $2.51 to $3.13 in the month after launch.

The Love Bear also sold itself better. Once visitors landed on its page, more of them added it to the cart:

Product pagePage → cartCart → shippingShipping → billingBilling → order
Mr. Fuzzy43.5%68.6%82.1%63.6%
Love Bear54.9%68.8%80.9%61.7%

After the cart, both products behave the same, so the difference comes from the product page itself. The Love Bear page could be a template for improving Mr. Fuzzy's.

Cross-selling on the cart page

In September 2013 the cart page started suggesting a second product. Comparing the month before and after:

PeriodCart sessionsCart → next stepProducts per orderAverage order valueRevenue per cart session
Before1,83067.2%1.00$51.42$18.32

The worry with cross-selling is that it distracts people and they leave. That did not happen: the click-through rate held steady and even rose slightly, while order value went up 5.5%.

Launch 2: the Birthday Panda

The third product had the clearest effect of any launch:

Every metric improved, and revenue per session rose by 21%. More products per order shows that customers were buying it alongside another toy, not just instead of one.

Which products sell together?

With four products, I built a cross-sell matrix: for each main product in an order, how often a second product was added.

Main productOrders+ Mini Bear+ Birthday Panda+ Love Bear
Mr. Fuzzy23,86113.1%7.4%3.7%
Love Bear4,80314.0%2.8%-
Birthday Panda3,06820.9%-3.7%

The Mini Bear is the natural add-on. It gets added to 13% to 21% of orders, most often with the Birthday Panda. The recommendation was to make it the default suggestion on the cart page.

A quality problem in the refunds

Mr. Fuzzy's refund rate usually stayed between 3% and 6%. In August and September 2014 it jumped to about 14%, while the other three products stayed below 2.5%. A spike that sudden, on one product only, points to a batch problem rather than a change in customers. After checking with the operations team, it was traced to a product quality problem, and by October the rate was back down to 2.7%. Watching refund rates by product and month catches this kind of problem early.

What the product analysis adds up to

Over three years, conversion rose from 3.0% to 7.7% and revenue per session from $1.52 to $4.93, more than three times as much. Better pages explain part of it. The rest came from the product range: each launch brought in new buyers, raised order value through cross-sells, and made the store less dependent on a single toy.

6. Customers: who comes back?

Of the 145,900 new visitors in 2014, about 13% came back at least once. Those who returned did so 33 days after their first visit on average (anywhere from 1 to 69 days).

Session typeSessionsConversion rateRevenue per session
New149,7876.80%$4.34
Repeat33,5778.11%$5.17

Returning visitors convert about 19% better and bring in about 19% more revenue per session. They also mostly arrive through direct visits, organic search and brand searches, which cost little or nothing. Paying full price to win them back through nonbrand ads would be wasted money.

Takeaways and next steps

  • Breaking an average down (by device, page or product) is where most insights came from. The campaign looked weak overall but was healthy on desktop.
  • The two page tests show the value of measuring a change properly: the same time window, the same traffic, and one clear metric.
  • Growth came from both more visitors and better conversion, and returning customers are the cheapest revenue the store has.
  • Next, I would build a retention campaign around the 30-day return window and track its effect with a holdout group.
